The Bankability Problem

Kenya needs KES 995 billion to reach its 2030 water and sanitation goals. On a business-as-usual path, roughly KES 71 billion of that has no identified source. The reason is not only a shortage of capital. Most of the utilities that would receive it cannot yet carry debt. Move the sliders below and you can see why.

The coverage model is a deliberately transparent, first-order approximation, calibrated so the sector-average inputs reproduce the sector's 98% cost coverage and the regulator's efficiency targets move it toward sustainability. It illustrates the mechanism and is not a substitute for a full utility financial model.

Why this matters

What you just did is, in miniature, the first thing I do when a water project lands on my desk. An applicant arrives with a financial model that shows the utility comfortably servicing the loan it is asking for. That model rests on assumptions: a metering ratio, a collection rate, a non-revenue water figure. My job is to ask whether those assumptions match what the utility has actually delivered.

Kenya is unusually well set up for that question, because the regulator publishes the answer. Its annual Impact Report scores every licensed utility on the same indicators, including the two you just moved. Three years of those reports read together give a trajectory that exists independently of anything the applicant has said.

The gap between where an applicant's projections sit on that gauge and where the regulator's data sits is usually the difference between a bankable transaction and one that will need concessional support at every debt service date. Finding that gap early protects scarce concessional capital from being deployed against numbers that were never going to hold.

---
title: Why MOIC should lead, but never alone, for patient capital
summary: IRR rewards speed, which is the wrong incentive for patient capital. But a time-blind metric needs time-aware companions.
date: 2026-09-28
topic: Valuation and exits
---

Internal rate of return is the default headline metric across private markets. Fund reports open with it, pitches lead with it, and carried interest is calculated on it. For commercial funds built around capital-efficient exits, that is mostly fine.

It becomes a problem when it is the headline metric for facilities whose purpose is the opposite. Blended finance, patient capital and much of development finance are defined by a willingness to hold longer than a commercial fund would. That is what makes the capital concessional in substance rather than in name.

## The mechanical problem with IRR

IRR is time-weighted, so every extra year of holding pulls it down. A 2x multiple delivered over three years is an IRR of about 26%. The same 2x over seven years is about 10%. The asset returned the same money to the same investors, but the headline number fell by more than half.

For a facility that promises patience, that creates a contradiction. It tells the world it holds longer than commercial peers, then leads with a metric that penalises exactly that. Every quarter its IRR sits below commercial benchmarks, someone asks why it is underperforming, when it is doing what it was designed to do.

## What MOIC says, and what it misses

Multiple on invested capital is time-blind: it tells you what a unit of capital became on the way out. A 2x is a 2x whether the hold was three years or ten, which is why it measures what patient capital is actually trying to do.

But time-blindness cuts both ways. A 1.1x returned over seven years is a loss in purchasing power, and for capital that is meant to be recycled, the speed at which money comes back decides how many more projects it can fund. A facility that leads with MOIC alone can hide slow recycling behind respectable multiples.

## The honest dashboard

So the argument is not MOIC instead of IRR. It is about which metric leads, and what sits beside it. For patient, recyclable capital I would lead with MOIC and carry two time-aware companions: the realised multiple to date (distributions to paid-in capital, or DPI), which shows how much has actually come back, and the average time to recycle, which shows how fast. IRR stays in the report, where committees and investors can find it.

Whichever metric leads becomes the metric everyone optimises for. Reporting decisions are strategy decisions.

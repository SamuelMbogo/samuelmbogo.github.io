title: "Layered facility, GP/LP fund or SMA: structure choices for a growing facility"
summary: For a facility seeking development finance capital for the first time, the choice of structure constrains everything downstream.
date: 2026-09-28

A blended finance facility that has proven its model with anchor donor capital eventually faces a familiar decision. To grow and bring in development finance institutions, it has to formalise its structure. Three options usually make the shortlist: a full general partner and limited partner (GP/LP) fund, a layered facility with concessional and commercial tranches, or a separately managed account (SMA) with one or more institutional partners.

They are not equivalent. Each carries different governance, capital-access and operational implications, and I have worked through this exact question for a facility planning its next phase.

## Full GP/LP fund

The most familiar structure in private markets, and the one that opens the door to institutional investors such as pension funds, endowments and sovereign funds, who often cannot invest through anything else. Development finance institutions are comfortable with it because it maps onto their internal templates.

The costs are substantial: a limited partnership agreement, fund administration, audit, investor reporting and usually a separate management company, plus regulatory obligations depending on where the fund sits. Critically for a first-time manager, many development finance institutions apply track-record thresholds that exclude first-time funds regardless of team quality. That alone can rule the route out for a facility without a long institutional history.

## Layered facility

A layered facility keeps the anchor donor's concessional capital in place and adds one or more commercial tranches above it, protected by the concessional layer taking first losses. It is classic blended finance applied at fund level rather than transaction level. It preserves the original mandate, keeps governance simpler than a full fund, and gives commercial investors downside protection they would not get elsewhere.

The trade-offs: layered structures can be harder to explain to investment committees that expect a familiar shape, and the first-loss economics have to work. The concessional layer must be large enough to protect the commercial tranches meaningfully, which ties the facility's minimum size to what the anchor donor will commit.

## Separately managed account

An SMA, in which the facility manages capital for a single institutional partner under a bespoke mandate, sits between the two. It avoids full fund-formation costs, keeps flexibility, and lets the facility build a real track record with an institutional counterparty. Several SMAs run consistently over time can become the practical equivalent of a fund track record, making a later GP/LP conversion much easier.

The limitation is scale. SMAs are bilateral, building several is operationally demanding, and they do not pool risk the way a fund does. They build capacity more than they define an end state.

## The pragmatic path

For a facility moving from proof of concept to institutional scale, my working view is that a layered facility or SMA fits better than an immediate GP/LP conversion. The GP/LP route becomes realistic once the track record and institutional infrastructure exist, which usually takes at least one more capital cycle. Jumping straight to a full fund without them tends to produce a vehicle that development finance institutions will not back and institutional investors will not commit to.

Structure is strategy. It decides what capital a facility can reach, what governance it takes on, and what its next options look like.

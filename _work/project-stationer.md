Regional manufacturers often have a financing problem that is really a presentation problem: operations spread across several countries, currencies and tax regimes, and no single set of numbers a financier can underwrite.

The client, a family-owned manufacturing and distribution group with a parent and seven subsidiaries across six countries in East and Southern Africa and the Indian Ocean, was part-way through a capital programme. A structured debt investor had already come in, and a development finance investor was being brought in for equity to fund expansion, product diversification and working capital.

My job was to keep the model behind that programme investable.

I analysed five years of financial statements for the parent and every subsidiary, then reviewed, updated and developed three-statement models for each entity and consolidated them into one group view, handling intercompany eliminations and translation across seven currencies. Debt capacity metrics sat on top (DSCR, interest cover and debt to EBITDA), because the model served two readers: the existing debt investor watching coverage and repayment, and the incoming equity investor focused on growth and governance.

Stress testing earned the credibility. I tested how one subsidiary's underperformance would ripple through the group, how currency shocks would hit a business whose key inputs are priced in dollars, and how the school calendar drives seasonal swings in working capital. I prepared management for investment committee questioning and worked directly with financiers.

The programme totals USD 12 million, split evenly between structured debt and equity. The equity leg closed in 2025 and funds the group's first move into a new product category with a clear social return.

## What I took from it

Debt and equity investors read the same model differently. A good model answers both without needing two versions of the truth.

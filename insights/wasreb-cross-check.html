Cross-Checking Water Utility Quick Scans Against WASREB Data

Applicants know how to make their projections look investable. The regulator's own data is where reality lives.

Samuel Mbogo · Nairobi · A note on due-diligence discipline

Every Quick Scan I've reviewed arrives with a base-case financial model that looks bankable. This is not a criticism of applicants. It is what a good sponsor does. They tune assumptions to the story they want to tell, and by the time the file reaches an investment analyst it has been through internal review, board sign-off, and probably a consultant's polish. The model works. It always works.

The question a due-diligence analyst has to answer is different: does it work at the assumptions the counterparty has actually delivered on historically. In Kenyan water infrastructure, we're lucky. That answer is publicly available, in a form regulators have specifically designed for comparison across utilities. It is called the Impact Report, published annually by the Water Services Regulatory Board (WASREB).

What the Impact Report actually contains

Each Impact Report scores every licensed water service provider in Kenya on nine key performance indicators, including drinking water quality, hours of supply, staff productivity, revenue collection efficiency, non-revenue water, metering ratio, personnel cost as a share of operating cost, and operation & maintenance cost coverage. It also publishes a Creditworthiness Index derived from these operational and financial metrics.

Three years of Impact Reports read together give you a trajectory. That trajectory is often the single most powerful piece of information you can bring into a Quick Scan review, because it exists independent of anything the applicant has told you, and it exists in a form the applicant themselves cannot dispute.

A worked example

In a recent review, an applicant utility submitted projections showing metering ratios of 87% and revenue collection efficiency of 94% in their base case, over a horizon of five years. These are aggressive but not implausible numbers for a well-run utility.

Cross-referenced against three consecutive Impact Reports, the actual figures told a different story. The metering ratio had oscillated between 62% and 71% over the three-year window. Collection efficiency had never exceeded 78%. The Creditworthiness Index had fallen by 15.8 points year-on-year in the most recent report, driven by declining O&M cost coverage and a data-reliability flag on volatile connection counts, which itself was worth naming as an issue.

None of this made the project undoable. It made the project a different project than the one submitted. A model built on 87% metering delivers materially different cashflow profiles from one built on 65%. That's not a rounding-error variance. That's the difference between an investable thesis and one that will require concessional subsidy at every debt-service point.

The recommendation that emerged was not to decline. It was to send the file back for clarification with a specific ask: rebuild the base case on the three-year historical median, provide the operational plan that closes the gap to the applicant's projected numbers, and treat the applicant's original figures as the upside scenario rather than the base.

Why this discipline matters

Concessional and blended-finance facilities exist precisely because commercial capital cannot fully price the risks in infrastructure projects like these. That is the design premise. But the concessional element is not free. It is scarce, it is contested, and every dollar of it deployed to a project that systematically overpromises is a dollar not available to a project that would have quietly delivered.

Regulator-published data is a discipline against the natural tendency to want deals to work. It doesn't kill projects. It sharpens them. And it is available, for free, to anyone who bothers to look.

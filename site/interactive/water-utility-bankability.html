The Bankability Problem

Kenya needs KES 995 billion to reach its 2030 water and sanitation goals. On a business-as-usual path, roughly KES 71 billion of that has no identified source. The reason isn't only a shortage of capital. It's that most of the utilities that would receive it can't yet carry debt. This is the problem I work on. Move the sliders below and you can see why.

Sector figures from WASREB's Impact Report 17 (FY 2023/24) and Kenya's NAWASIP investment plan. The coverage model is a deliberately transparent, first-order approximation, calibrated so the sector average inputs reproduce the sector's real 98% cost coverage and the regulator's efficiency targets move it toward sustainability. It is an illustration of the mechanism, not a substitute for a full utility financial model.

What you just did is, in miniature, the first thing I do when a water project lands on my desk for review. An applicant arrives with a financial model that shows the utility comfortably servicing the loan it's asking for. That model is built on assumptions: a metering ratio, a collection rate, a non-revenue-water figure. My job is to ask whether those assumptions match what the utility has actually delivered.

Kenya is unusually well set up for that question, because the regulator publishes the answer. WASREB's annual Impact Report scores every licensed utility on the same nine indicators, including the two you just moved. Three years of those reports read together give a trajectory that exists independently of anything the applicant has told me, in a form the applicant can't dispute.

The gap between the two positions on that gauge, where an applicant's projections sit versus where the regulator's data sits, is usually the difference between a bankable transaction and one that will quietly require concessional subsidy at every debt-service point. Finding that gap early is what protects scarce concessional capital from being deployed against numbers that were never going to hold.

Why MOIC Beats IRR as the Honest Metric in Patient Capital

IRR rewards speed. If you're deploying patient capital by design, that's not the incentive you want your reporting to reinforce.

Samuel Mbogo · Nairobi · A note on portfolio metrics

Internal Rate of Return (IRR) is the default headline return metric across most private markets. Every LP report starts with it. Every fund pitch leads with it. Every carry calculation is anchored to it. This is fine, mostly, for commercial funds where the entire premise is finding capital-efficient exits.

It becomes a problem when it becomes the headline metric for facilities whose entire premise is the opposite. Blended finance, patient capital, and impact-oriented development finance are all defined by their willingness to hold assets for longer than a commercial fund would. That's the whole point. It's what makes the capital concessional in a substantive rather than nominal sense.

The mechanical problem with IRR

IRR is a time-weighted return. Every additional year of holding drags the number down. A 2x MOIC delivered over three years generates a roughly 26% IRR. The same 2x MOIC over seven years generates roughly 10%. Nothing about the underlying value has changed. The asset returned the same money, to the same investors, at the same cost of capital. But the headline number has fallen by more than half.

For a fund that markets patient capital, this creates an internal contradiction. The fund tells the world it holds longer than commercial peers, then reports a headline metric that penalises exactly that behaviour. Every quarter that IRR sits below commercial-fund IRRs, someone will ask why the fund is underperforming. The honest answer is that the fund is doing exactly what it was designed to do. But that answer is much harder to make convincingly when the metric is telling a different story.

What Multiple on Invested Capital says

Multiple on Invested Capital (MOIC) is time-blind. It tells you what a dollar in became on the way out. A 2x MOIC is a 2x MOIC whether the holding period was three years or ten. That's not a bug, that's the feature. For a fund whose returns compound through patient deployment rather than rapid recycling, MOIC is the metric that measures what the fund is actually trying to do.

This isn't an argument against reporting IRR. Committees will still want it. LPs will still ask for it. That's fine. The argument is about which metric leads. Which metric appears above the fold, in the quarterly report, in the pitch deck, in the internal dashboard. Because whichever metric leads becomes the metric everyone optimises for.

The behavioural point

In one facility I work with, we recently adopted MOIC as the headline portfolio return metric. The practical effect is subtle but real. Investment decisions that would have been marginal on an IRR basis, because the projected hold period was long, now look attractive on a MOIC basis where the projected multiple is strong. The projects that are getting through are the projects the facility was designed to support. That alignment matters more than the theoretical purity of any given number.

IRR is not the wrong metric. It's the wrong headline metric for anyone whose thesis is measured in decades rather than quarters. Reporting decisions are strategy decisions. Choose accordingly.

Layered Facility, GP/LP, or SMA: Fund-Form Choices for Concessional Anchors

For a first-time fund pulling DFI capital, the fund-form decision constrains everything downstream. Here's how the three options actually compare.

Samuel Mbogo · Nairobi · A note on fund structuring

A blended-finance facility that has demonstrated its model with anchor donor capital eventually faces a familiar decision. To scale further and access development finance institution (DFI) capital, it needs to formalise its structure. The three options that typically make the shortlist are a full GP/LP conversion, a layered facility (with concessional and commercial tranches), or a Separately Managed Account (SMA) arrangement with one or more institutional partners.

These options are not equivalent. Each carries different governance, capital-access, and operational-complexity implications. Below is how they actually stack up, based on structural analysis I've done on this exact question for a facility pursuing its next capital raise.

Full GP/LP conversion

The most familiar structure in private markets. Attractive because it opens the door to institutional LPs (pension funds, endowments, sovereign wealth) who cannot invest through anything else. Also the structure DFIs are most comfortable with, because it maps cleanly onto their internal investment templates.

The costs are substantial. Full LPA drafting, fund administration, audit, LP reporting infrastructure, and typically a management company standing up separately from the fund. Regulatory obligations depending on jurisdiction. And, critically for a first-time fund, a track record requirement that many DFIs apply. One DFI I spoke to was explicit that they do not consider first-time funds regardless of team quality, and that constraint alone can rule this route out for facilities without a decade of institutional history.

Layered facility

A layered facility keeps the anchor donor's concessional tranche in place and adds one or more senior commercial tranches on top, protected by the concessional first-loss layer. This is the classic blended finance structure at the fund level rather than the transaction level. It preserves the original mandate, keeps governance simpler than a full GP/LP, and gives commercial investors risk protection that they would not receive in an unblended vehicle.

The trade-off is that layered facilities can be difficult to explain to institutional LPs whose investment committees expect a familiar shape. And the first-loss economics have to actually work: the concessional tranche has to be large enough to give commercial investors meaningful downside protection, which anchors the facility's minimum size to whatever the anchor donor is willing to commit.

Separately Managed Account (SMA)

An SMA arrangement, where a facility manages capital on behalf of a single institutional partner through a bespoke mandate, sits between the two. It preserves flexibility, avoids full fund-formation costs, and lets the facility build a real track record with an institutional counterparty. It also creates optionality: several SMAs, run consistently over time, become the substantive equivalent of a fund track record, and can support a later GP/LP conversion with much less friction.

The limitation is scale. SMAs are typically bilateral. Building a portfolio of SMAs is possible but operationally intensive, and it does not create the same pooled-risk dynamic as a fund. It's a capacity-building structure more than an end-state one.

The pragmatic path

For facilities in the transition from proof-of-concept to institutional scale, my working view is that layered facility or SMA models fit better than a full GP/LP conversion. The GP/LP option becomes viable once track record and institutional infrastructure are in place, which usually takes at least one more capital cycle. Attempting to jump straight to full fund structure without those preconditions typically results in a fund that DFIs will not back and institutional LPs will not commit to.

Fund-form decisions are not just technical. They are strategic. They determine what capital the facility can access, what governance obligations it takes on, and what its next-cycle options look like. The right answer depends on where the facility is in its evolution, not on which structure looks best on paper.
